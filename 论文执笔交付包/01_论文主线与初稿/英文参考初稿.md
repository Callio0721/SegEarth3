# Joint Role–View Specialization for Training-Free Open-Vocabulary Remote Sensing Segmentation

*First draft: Related Work, Method, and Experiments. Citation numbers refer to [references.md](references.md). Unavailable comparisons are marked “—”; they are not reported experiments.*

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1. Open-Vocabulary Dense Prediction

Vision–language pretraining enables recognition with a vocabulary specified through text rather than a fixed classifier [1]. Transferring this capability to segmentation requires spatially resolved predictions. Training-free methods address this gap by reorganizing pretrained representations: LPOSS propagates labels over patches and pixels [2], CASS transfers object-context structure through spectral graph matching [3], and PEARL combines attention-space alignment with text-aware propagation [4]. Alternatively, trained approaches improve region–text correspondence, as in Mask-Adapter [5], or reorganize spatial and semantic cost aggregation, as in PCA-Seg [17]. These methods demonstrate that strong global recognition does not by itself determine how semantic evidence should be used for dense prediction.

Remote sensing introduces additional variation in object scale, appearance, and label semantics. SegEarth-OV adapts CLIP through feature upsampling and global-bias mitigation [6]; ReAttnCLIP separately revises patch interactions and the influence of global tokens [7]. SkySense-O instead develops a vision-centric model through remote-sensing pretraining and fine-tuning [21]. Our work follows the frozen-model setting, but starts from a concept-grounded segmentor with native dense and query-based outputs rather than adapting a global image–text similarity model.

### 2.2. Prompt Adaptation and Concept-Grounded Segmentation

Textual equivalence does not guarantee equivalent visual grounding. SynCLIP explicitly studies inconsistent spatial responses to synonymous expressions [20]. For remote sensing, TMPA generates context-aware descriptions and adapts their CLIP embeddings using high-confidence visual features and test-time entropy optimization [8]. ConceptBank constructs representative labeled support sets, evaluates candidate concepts by their induced segmentation quality, and combines high-scoring embeddings into a concept bank [9]. These approaches motivate evaluating a prompt by what it makes the model do, rather than only by its linguistic plausibility.

SAM 3 introduces promptable concept segmentation and separates concept recognition from localization through a presence head [10]. SegEarth-OV3 repurposes its semantic, instance, and presence outputs for remote-sensing OVSS [11]. ProC-SAM3 constructs a constrained prompt pool and preserves semantic predictions while adding a gated, nonnegative instance advantage [12]. CoCo-SAM3 aggregates synonymous semantic evidence and calibrates competition between independently prompted categories [13]. Our focus is different: supplementary descriptions need not serve all native outputs equally. We retain complete concept grounding and specialize the subsequent use of its outputs according to their computational roles. The distinction is role-conditioned evidence use, not a claim that longer descriptions or three non-anchor prompts are universally preferable.

### 2.3. Complementary Visual Observations

Local detail and spatial context have long motivated complementary observation paths. HRDA combines high-resolution detail crops and lower-resolution context predictions through learned scale attention [18]. SegMAN models local and global dependencies within a segmentation network [23], while F2Net separates frequency components for high-resolution remote-sensing segmentation [19]. In training-free OVSS, GLA-CLIP exchanges information across windows by extending attention keys and values [14]; OV-Stitcher combines cross-window attention stitching with class-biased textual prompts [15]. DPSeg uses generated visual prompts alongside textual prompts in cost-volume learning [16], whereas MM-OVSeg obtains additional evidence from registered optical and SAR imagery [22].

We use neither an auxiliary visual modality nor a modified attention operator. We obtain aligned observations from RGB imagery, ground each observation with the same frozen model, and combine their class-score fields. Our central question is how the usefulness of these observations changes after role-specific textual supplementation. This connects language specialization and visual context through their measured interaction, rather than treating multi-view inference as an independent add-on.

## 3. Method

### 3.1. Overview and Native Concept Grounding

Given an RGB image and a set of category names \(\mathcal C\), open-vocabulary semantic segmentation assigns each evaluated pixel to a category in \(\mathcal C\), with background handled by the benchmark protocol. We propose **Joint Role–View Specialization (JRV)**, a training-free framework built on SegEarth-OV3 [11]. It addresses two related limitations: a shared category prompt does not necessarily provide suitable supplementary information for every output role, and a single observation scale does not necessarily expose the visual evidence required by that information.

JRV first forms anchor-preserving, role-specialized predictions for each observation. It then aligns and combines local and context predictions. All SAM 3 weights remain frozen. Every textual input undergoes the complete native grounding computation; specialization is applied to the use of its outputs, not by isolating internal attention states.

**Native outputs.** For one category and one observation, omitting their indices for clarity, let

\[
\Phi(X,t)=\big(p,S,\mathcal Q\big),\qquad
\mathcal Q=\{(M_q,r_q)\}_{q=1}^{Q}. \tag{1}
\]

Here \(p\) is concept-presence confidence, \(S(x)\) is a dense semantic score, and each object query produces a mask probability \(M_q(x)\) and an object score \(r_q\). These outputs share concept-conditioned computation. Presence evaluates whether a concept is supported; the semantic branch supplies dense coverage; the instance branch organizes evidence into query-specific regions. A query region can represent an extended surface as well as a compact object.

In the baseline implementation, instance aggregation and final fusion can be expressed as

\[
\mathcal I(\mathcal Q;p,a)(x)
=\max_{q:\,a r_q>\eta}M_q(x)r_qp,
\qquad
F(p,S,\mathcal Q;a)(x)
=p\max\!\left(S(x),\mathcal I(\mathcal Q;p,a)(x)\right), \tag{2}
\]

where the maximum over an empty query set is zero. Native admission uses \(a=p\). The separately specified \(a\) also allows anchor-based query admission while retaining presence modulation of instance amplitude. Equation (2) makes the roles' coupling explicit: changing presence can alter query membership, instance strength, and final class competition. Consequently, improving a role in isolation need not yield the same gain when other roles change.

### 3.2. Role-Functional Text Supplementation

**Descriptions organized by function.** For each category \(c\), we retain a canonical anchor \(t_c^A\) and construct small candidate sets for the three roles. Presence candidates emphasize concept naming or existence-compatible noun phrases; semantic candidates describe appearance and spatial coverage; instance candidates describe region extent, separability, or organization. An LLM generates these candidates from the category vocabulary and its dataset meaning. They are short category descriptions, not new classes or pixel-level instructions.

For example, the recorded Potsdam road bank contains the anchor “road,” the presence candidate “roads,” the semantic candidate “continuous road surface,” and the instance candidate “complete connected road network.” These illustrate different intended functions; the selected Potsdam configuration retains the instance anchor. Candidate language specifies a hypothesis about useful evidence, not an enforced topology constraint.

A role profile selects one candidate index for each role, consistently across the benchmark's category bank. Index zero retains the anchor. For a selected triple \(\boldsymbol\pi=(\pi_P,\pi_S,\pi_I)\), we obtain the corresponding outputs from complete grounding runs. We do not assume that a semantic-oriented prompt leaves the other native outputs unchanged. Instead, its semantic output is the one admitted into the supplementary semantic path. The same principle applies to presence and instance evidence.

**Bounded, anchor-relative updates.** Define

\[
\mathcal R(a,b)
=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}
\left[a+\alpha\operatorname{clip}_{[-\tau,\tau]}(b-a)\right]. \tag{3}
\]

The presence and semantic updates are

\[
\widetilde p=\mathcal R(p_A,p_{\pi_P}),
\qquad
\widetilde S=\mathcal R(S_A,S_{\pi_S}). \tag{4}
\]

Instance evidence must be reconstructed under the updated presence rather than treated as an independent map. We therefore use

\[
\begin{aligned}
I_A^{\widetilde p}&=\mathcal I(\mathcal Q_A;\widetilde p,a),\\
I_{\pi_I}^{\widetilde p}&=\mathcal I(\mathcal Q_{\pi_I};\widetilde p,a),\\
\widetilde I&=\mathcal R(I_A^{\widetilde p},I_{\pi_I}^{\widetilde p}),
\qquad
F_{\boldsymbol\pi}=\widetilde p\max(\widetilde S,\widetilde I).
\end{aligned} \tag{5}
\]

Both query sets use the same admission policy: \(a=\widetilde p\) for native admission or \(a=p_A\) for anchor admission. Masks are aggregated before the residual update, so no correspondence between anchor and supplementary query identities is assumed.

This construction separates **what information is supplemented** while preserving **how native outputs interact**. Each explicit update in Equation (3) is bounded by \(\alpha\tau\), and an unchanged candidate gives zero update. Query membership can still change under native admission. We use these properties to limit displacement from the anchor, not to assume that every supplementary response is correct.

**Preserving the official prediction.** Some baseline categories use multiple names or aliases. Let \(B\) denote the score field returned by the official baseline processing, and let \(F_A\) be the corresponding structural-anchor composition without supplementation. The observation-level prediction is

\[
G_{\boldsymbol\pi}
=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}
\left[B+F_{\boldsymbol\pi}-F_A\right]. \tag{6}
\]

Thus, disabling all role updates returns \(B\) exactly, including its original alias handling. This is not an ensemble of three complete candidate predictions: the supplementary outputs interact through Equation (5), and only their change relative to the structural anchor is added to the official result.

### 3.3. Complementary Local and Context Observations

Text can specify a continuous surface or a complete region, but it cannot supply visual content absent from the observation. Conversely, a wider observation may expose surrounding structure while reducing the spatial detail allocated to a small object. The detail–context trade-off is explicit in multi-resolution segmentation [18,19] and in analyses of independent-window inference [14].

A simple sampling argument makes the connection concrete. For a square field of width \(L\), resized to \(R\) input pixels with token stride \(s\), an object spanning \(d\) source pixels occupies approximately \(dR/(Ls)\) tokens along one axis. Increasing \(L\) exposes more context but reduces that object's token footprint. A smaller crop reverses the trade-off. Neither operation creates new sensor detail: they redistribute the model's spatial sampling and accessible context.

We therefore construct a local observation set \(\mathcal V_L\) and a larger-context set \(\mathcal V_G\). Local crops preserve a larger effective sampling density; context observations cover a larger portion of the same scene. Each crop is processed independently using Equations (1)–(6), including complete concept grounding. Its continuous class scores are then mapped back to the target coordinate system. For view family \(v\), overlapping predictions are combined as

\[
\overline G_v(x)
=\frac{\sum_{u\in\mathcal V_v}w_u(x)\,
\mathcal W_u[G_{\boldsymbol\pi,u}](x)}
{\sum_{u\in\mathcal V_v}w_u(x)}, \tag{7}
\]

where \(\mathcal W_u\) restores scale and coordinates, and \(w_u\) indicates valid target coverage. A context observation contributes only its geometrically corresponding target region. We combine continuous scores, not independently discretized crop labels.

The two view families use the **same selected role profile**. The method does not impose “semantic is global” or “instance is local”: extended query regions can need context, while dense semantic prediction can benefit from detail. The output roles are specialized by their supplementary text, and their usefulness is evaluated under complementary observations.

### 3.4. Joint Role–View Composition

The final class-score field is

\[
Z_c(x)=\Psi_{\omega}
\left(\overline G_{L,c}(x),\overline G_{G,c}(x)\right),
\qquad
\widehat Y=\mathcal D_{\mathrm{base}}(Z), \tag{8}
\]

where \(\mathcal D_{\mathrm{base}}\) retains the baseline's category decision and background thresholding. \(\Psi_{\omega}\) is a fixed, class-wise view composition operator, not a learned gate. We consider endpoint selection, element-wise maximum, weighted means in score or log-odds space, and power means. Their definitions and selected settings are given in Appendix A.

The reason to consider role and view profiles jointly is that SAM 3 grounds language against the available visual field: changing the observation can change both a supplementary prompt's response and its interaction with the anchor. The useful role profile under one observation need not remain optimal after view composition. We select a fixed joint configuration per benchmark and use it unchanged for all its images; there is no image-wise ground-truth routing.

This gives a specific explanation for possible synergy. Role supplementation changes the semantic evidence available in each observation, while view composition changes which complementary responses can influence the final category decision. Positive interaction is expected when these changes correct errors that neither component resolves alone. It is not guaranteed: correlated mistakes, overlapping corrections, or degradation of a strong single-view prediction can reduce the benefit. Section 4.4 tests this explanation using matched component configurations rather than assuming additive gains.

## 4. Experiments

### 4.1. Experimental Setup

**Benchmarks and metrics.** We evaluate on eight remote-sensing benchmarks used by SegEarth-OV and SegEarth-OV3 [6,11]: Potsdam, Vaihingen, LoveDA, iSAID, OpenEarthMap (OEM), UAVid, UDD5, and VDD. We retain the baseline's evaluated splits, class mappings, ignored labels, and image-based UAVid protocol. The iSAID evaluation includes 11,644 processed validation tiles; UAVid includes 1,020 evaluated patches. Mean intersection over union (mIoU, %) is the primary metric. The overall average weights the eight datasets equally. Class-group analysis instead weights dataset–class pairs equally and is reported separately. VOC20 and Cityscapes provide additional natural-image evaluations.

**Implementation and configuration.** We use the same frozen SAM 3 checkpoint and native grounding as the reproduced baseline. Candidate descriptions are generated offline, with selected descriptions stored verbatim. Unless specified otherwise, \(\alpha=0.5\) and \(\tau=0.25\). Role candidates and view settings are compared using the available evaluation annotations, and the selected fixed profiles are reported; these are evaluation-selected results, not results from an independent held-out configuration-selection split. No SAM 3 weights are optimized, and inference with a fixed profile does not use annotations. Appendix A lists the selected profiles.

Local and context observations come from the input RGB image, except that Potsdam and Vaihingen use spatially aligned neighboring RGB tiles from the same original scene to reconstruct a larger context field. Only the target tile is scored. The standard configuration uses 512-pixel local and 1,024-pixel context fields for these two datasets. Their input extent is therefore larger than a target-tile-only evaluation; we additionally report that setting in Appendix B. No temporal frames, labeled visual exemplars, or auxiliary sensing modalities are introduced.

**Comparison sources.** Competitor numbers in Table 1 are transcribed from the ProC-SAM3 comparison [12]; the baseline row follows our established reproduced comparison reference. All component gains and interaction statistics use the unrounded, matched baseline in Table 3. These two baseline records are kept distinct rather than mixing rounded comparison values into paired ablations.

### 4.3. Why Specialize the Use of Supplementary Text?

**The same description can have different utility across roles.** We compare using a semantic-oriented candidate only for semantic supplementation against using that same candidate for all three roles. Both retain the anchor and the bounded update mechanism. Table 2 presents one recorded candidate setting per dataset, including an opposing case. Across all 18 tested dataset–candidate settings, semantic-only use is better in 13 cases; shared use improves over the baseline in 7 cases.

**Table 2. A matched description, two uses.** Values are final segmentation mIoU, not standalone head accuracy. The candidate index refers to the recorded semantic prompt bank; both columns use the same candidate within a row. The complete 18-setting table is retained in the supplementary evidence.

| Dataset | Candidate | Baseline | Semantic-only use | Shared P/S/I use |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Potsdam | S2 | 57.87 | **58.00** | 56.63 |
| Vaihingen | S3 | 60.86 | **61.57** | 61.28 |
| LoveDA | S1 | 47.34 | **48.09** | 46.61 |
| OEM | S3 | 44.30 | **46.28** | 44.02 |
| UDD5 | S3 | 71.80 | 71.56 | **73.04** |
| VDD | S1 | 64.11 | **65.27** | 65.05 |

On OEM, the same S3 descriptions produce 46.28 when used for semantic supplementation but 44.02 when shared across roles. On LoveDA, semantic-only use improves the baseline, whereas shared use lowers it. UDD5 exhibits the reverse behavior. The conclusion is therefore not that shared prompts invariably fail; it is that prompt utility depends on its computational use. This motivates retaining the anchor as an available choice instead of requiring every role to accept new text.

**Preserving the anchor matters.** With final role/view settings fixed, the anchor-preserving strategy outperforms the recorded direct-prediction strategy on five of the six datasets with completed comparisons (Appendix B). For example, Joint mIoU changes from 30.99 to 48.81 on LoveDA and from 39.37 to 53.08 on OEM. VDD is an exception, where direct prediction reaches 67.26 versus 66.55. This comparison evaluates the complete anchor-preserving strategy, including official-score compensation; it does not isolate clipping alone. The evidence favors a stable semantic reference as the default, while allowing dataset-dependent exceptions.

### 4.4. Do Role and View Contributions Reinforce Each Other?

To distinguish improvement from genuine interaction, we evaluate four conditions with the final role, admission, and view choices held fixed: baseline \(m_{00}\), role supplementation only \(m_{10}\), view composition only \(m_{01}\), and both \(m_{11}\). We measure the performance interaction

\[
\Gamma=m_{11}-m_{10}-m_{01}+m_{00}. \tag{9}
\]

This is a factorial contrast in mIoU, not an interaction-pixel count. It tests whether the joint performance gain exceeds the sum of the two matched individual gains.

**Table 3. Matched Role × View ablation.** The role-only column uses the final joint role configuration, not an independently reselected role-only optimum. All values are computed from unrounded results before display.

| Dataset | Baseline \(m_{00}\) | Role \(m_{10}\) | View \(m_{01}\) | Joint \(m_{11}\) | \(\Gamma\) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Potsdam | 57.87 | 58.59 | 62.34 | **63.18** | +0.11 |
| Vaihingen | 60.86 | 61.59 | 63.58 | **66.38** | +2.07 |
| LoveDA | 47.34 | 48.30 | 47.18 | **48.81** | +0.66 |
| iSAID | 27.64 | 28.27 | 28.73 | **29.13** | −0.23 |
| OEM | 44.30 | 50.53 | 45.17 | **53.08** | +1.68 |
| UAVid | 54.58 | 59.50 | 54.43 | **59.82** | +0.47 |
| UDD5 | 71.80 | 72.76 | 72.27 | **72.94** | −0.28 |
| VDD | 64.11 | 65.83 | 64.30 | **66.55** | +0.53 |
| **Average** | **53.56** | **55.67** | **54.75** | **57.49** | **+0.63** |

The joint configuration is better than either matched component on all eight datasets, and interaction is positive on six. The strongest interactions occur on Vaihingen (+2.07) and OEM (+1.68). LoveDA and UAVid provide particularly direct evidence of conditional usefulness: their selected view operator slightly degrades the anchor prediction (−0.16 and −0.15), yet improves the corresponding role-specialized prediction (+0.50 and +0.32). Thus, the benefit cannot be explained solely by adding a uniformly stronger visual prediction.

Potsdam behaves differently: view information already provides most of the improvement, while extra interaction is only +0.11. On iSAID and UDD5, the joint prediction improves over both individual components, but their gains overlap, yielding negative interaction. These cases agree with the distinction in Section 3.4 between complementary and redundant corrections. Positive interaction establishes conditional usefulness at the performance level; identifying particular repaired boundaries or query events requires the corresponding spatial evidence.

Allowing role-only profiles to be selected independently gives 55.82 average mIoU. JRV still improves this stronger reference by 1.67 points, reaching 57.49. We keep this comparison separate from Table 3 because independently selected profiles do not define a matched factorial contrast.

JRV improves 48 of 63 dataset–class pairs, including 41 of 55 foreground pairs. Vegetation and land-cover classes show the broadest consistency, with 14 of 15 positive pairs. Water-related and built-structure categories also improve on average. This pattern is compatible with the intended combination of coverage-oriented language and observations that expose larger spatial organization. Movable objects gain little from role text alone on average, but improve after joint composition, showing that the two components need not benefit the same category groups equally.

The effect is not a universal continuity prior. Transport surfaces improve over the baseline but slightly decline relative to role-only prediction. Background/clutter exhibits a large mean gain influenced by OEM; its group median is only +1.56. The foreground results therefore provide a more informative account of broad semantic improvement than the background mean alone.

Pixel transitions confirm that these gains involve actual corrected assignments. Across the evaluated remote-sensing pixels, approximately 429.16 million baseline errors become correct, compared with 165.85 million previously correct pixels becoming incorrect—a net increase of 263.31 million correct assignments. These are counts over evaluated images/tiles, not independent geographic samples. They complement class-balanced mIoU and do not imply that every class or individual component improves pixel accuracy.
